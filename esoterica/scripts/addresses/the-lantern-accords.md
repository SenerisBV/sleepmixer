And so they met here. On this hill, in a barn that stood where this hall now stands, with the snow banked up against its doors and every window lit. The delegates of the Nine Shores. Fishers and bee-keepers and lock-keepers and the two old men from the glass-works at Orrin who would not sit down because they said they had been sitting down all their lives. [laughs quietly] They stood for three days. It is in the minutes.

And they wrote the Accords. By lamplight. On the backs of grain tallies, because that was the paper they had.

You will hear the articles read later tonight, in full, as they are every Long Evening, and I will not take that from the reader. But I want to speak of three of them. Only three.

The fourth article: that no window facing a road shall be dark on the Long Evening, so long as there is oil anywhere on the Nine Shores. [pause] Anywhere. Not in your house. Anywhere. Your window is lit by every jar on every shore, and every jar on every shore is lit by your window.

The seventh article: that there shall be, in every town, a house of listening. Where anyone may come in out of the dark and sit, and say what is on them, and be heard, and not be answered unless they ask to be. We have kept that one. Some of you have sat in those rooms. Some of you have been the one who listens. You know that it is the hardest work there is and the quietest.

[breath]

And the ninth article. The one the children laugh at, and should. The one the scholars argue over, and should. That on the Long Evening no one shall be required to finish what they were saying.

[long pause]

I have been asked, many times, what the delegates meant by that. I have read every page of the minutes, and I will tell you. They do not say. The minutes say only that the ninth article was proposed by the lock-keeper from Senn, late on the third night, and that the barn was quiet for a long time, and then it was agreed without a vote.

I think I know what they meant. I am old enough now to think I know.

I think they had been talking for three days. I think they had argued about oil, and roads, and who would carry what, and they had got it right. And I think, near the end, someone in that barn began to say something, something about the dark, or about the sun, or about the people walking on the roads with jars on their backs. And halfway through, they stopped. Not because they had nothing to say. Because they had found, halfway through, that it was already said. That the room had understood it before they got there.

And the lock-keeper from Senn looked round at all those faces in the lamplight and thought: that should be allowed. That should always be allowed.

[pause]

So we allow it. On this night of all nights. You may begin a thing and leave it. You may set it down in the middle like a jar on a step, and someone will come along and carry it the rest of the way. Or no one will, and it will sit there in the lamplight being half of something, and that will be enough. That will be, on this night, exactly enough.

[breath]

This hall is built of stone from all nine shores. Grey from Harrowmere, and the red from Vell, and the pale soft stone from the salt towns that you can mark with your thumbnail. The masons tell me it will stand five hundred Long Evenings. I believe them. But I want to say to the masons, and to the joiners, and to the glaziers who made those windows, those very tall windows on the road side: it is not the stone. It was never the stone. It is the light in the windows. And the light in the windows is only ever as old as tonight.

[passage]

In a little while the bell will ring and we will go out. You know how it goes. We will go out into the square with our jars, the little ones, the ones your grandparents gave you, and we will walk the roads out of Tessary a short way, and we will set a light on the wall of someone we do not know.

And then we will come home. And we will sit by our windows. And we will not finish what we were saying.

I want to tell you about my grandmother's grandmother, the one with the bees. She kept a lamp in her window that whole first Long Evening, and on the second night a man came up the hill road carrying a jar on his back and a hive under his arm.
