eighteen minutes past the low bell, and this is QVEL, news on the far shore, all through the dark.

Our top stories this hour. [pause] The Harbour Council has voted to keep the streetlamps along the Esplanade at half-glow until morning, after a week of record arrivals from the near side. Council members said most new arrivals continue to come in asleep, and that half-glow was felt to be, quote, kinder on the eyes. The lamplighters' guild has welcomed the decision.

The Office of Mislaid Hours reports another busy night. More than four thousand afternoons were handed in at the counter on Tallow Street since sundown, most of them in good condition, several still warm. If you believe you may have mislaid an afternoon, the office asks that you come in person and be ready to describe the weather in it.

And in the Murmuring Quarter, residents are being asked to keep their windows open this evening for the passing of the paper birds. The flock was last reported over the salt marshes heading east, and is expected over the rooftops some time after the second moon. Officials remind listeners that the birds are not to be caught, and that any that come in through a window should simply be allowed to rest and then shown the way out.

[pause]

Weather and the crossings in a moment. First, the time. It's nineteen minutes past the low bell. [breath] This is QVEL.

QVEL weather, on the eights.

Overnight on the far shore, a soft luminous drizzle, mostly over the Stillwater and the lower town, easing toward the hour of moths. Light winds from the near side, bringing that faint smell of toast and wet pavement listeners will know well by now. Temperatures holding at about the warmth of a hand. The second moon rises at eleven past the bell over Carrow Spit, waxing, a little blue. Visibility on the Stillwater is down to two lanterns in places, so take your time out there.

Tomorrow, more of the same. Long light, low cloud, a chance of hearing your name called from a great distance in the late afternoon. Nothing to worry about.

[pause]

Now the crossings. [pause] The ferry from the near side is running about fifteen minutes behind the moon tonight, owing to heavy dreaming on the outbound shore. The ferrymen ask passengers to remain seated, keep their hands inside the boat, and not to mind if the oars sound like someone asleep beside them, breathing slow. That is normal for this time of night.

On the Long Stair, slow going on the upper flights, where a number of travellers have stopped to look back over the rail. Marshals are on hand and are not hurrying anybody.

The Bridge of Small Forgettings has one lane open in each direction while the planks are oiled. If you find you've forgotten why you set out, you're advised to keep walking. It usually comes back on the far side.

And the tram along the Esplanade is running normally, every few minutes, or whenever you happen to be standing at the stop.

That's the crossings. Thirty past the low bell. QVEL.

Around the far shore tonight. [pause]

In the lower town, the bakers on Cinder Row have begun their overnight shift, and the first loaves are expected out at the usual time. As always, the bakers will be leaving a basket on the step of the house of listening for anyone who's still up. Rye and seed tonight, we're told. And a few of the little round ones.

The lighthouse keeper at Carrow Spit reports a quiet evening. She says the light is turning well and the lens has been cleaned, and she'd like to thank the listener who sent up the new blanket. She says it's very warm. She says it's blue.

At the Hall of Unsent Letters, volunteers have finished sorting the evening's post. Most letters this week were addressed to old houses, to people at old addresses, to someone who used to live at the end of a road. Volunteers will read a selection aloud in the reading room tomorrow from the hour of moths. Admission is free. Bring a cushion.

[breath]

And a note from the orchards above the town. Pickers say the lamp-apples are ripening early this season, and are asking anyone walking the high rows after dusk not to shake the trees. They come down on their own when they're ready.

In community notices: the choir at the Fishmarket Steps is looking for low voices for the long hum. No experience required. Just turn up, they say, and hum what you hear.

[pause]

Thirty-eight past the low bell. You're listening to QVEL.

For anyone out on the water tonight: calm, calm, calm. The buoys off the spit are ringing gently. The fishing boats are in. The heron on the breakwater has been there since sundown and shows no sign of going anywhere.
